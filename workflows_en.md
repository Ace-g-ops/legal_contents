# Nigerian Criminal Justice Process — Stage-by-Stage Workflow

## Stage: Arrest

**What happens:**
A police officer (or in some cases another law enforcement agency) takes a person into custody based on a court order, reasonable suspicion of a crime, or another lawful ground. The person is told they are under arrest, and — unless it's a life-threatening emergency — should be told why. Physical restraint should only be as much as necessary; excessive force is not permitted. This is the starting point of the entire criminal justice process, and most of the rights that follow are designed to protect the person from this moment onward.

**What you should expect:**
- Who will be there: one or more police officers, possibly plainclothes officers depending on the unit
- What questions you'll be asked: basic identification (name, address) at minimum; more detailed questioning usually happens at the station, not on the street
- What decisions might be made: whether to take you directly to the station, whether handcuffs/restraint are used, whether you're informed of the specific offence on the spot or told "we'll explain at the station"

**Your rights at this stage:**
- Right against arbitrary arrest — there must be a lawful ground (Section 35(1))
- Right to be informed of the reason for arrest, in a language you understand (Section 35(3), Section 36(6)(a))
- Right to remain silent (Section 35(2))
- Right to humane treatment — no excessive force (Section 34(1))

**What you can say:**
- "What am I being arrested for?"
- "I don't understand English well — please explain in [Yoruba/Hausa/Pidgin]."
- "I wish to remain silent until I've spoken to a lawyer."

## Stage: At the Police Station (Investigation & Statement)

**What happens:**
Once at the station, the suspect may be questioned, searched, and asked to make a statement about the alleged offence. This is where most of the rights violations people worry about tend to happen — statements taken under pressure, delays in access to a lawyer, or being held longer than legally allowed. If the offence qualifies, the police may grant "police bail" here without needing to go to court at all.

**What you should expect:**
- Who will be there: investigating officer(s), possibly a lawyer if one has been requested and arrives, occasionally an interpreter
- What questions you'll be asked: details of the alleged offence, sometimes about your background, associates, or whereabouts at relevant times
- What decisions might be made: whether to release you on police bail, whether to refer the case for prosecution, whether to keep you in custody pending court

**Your rights at this stage:**
- Right to an interpreter if you don't understand the language being used (Section 36(6)(e))
- Right to a lawyer of your choice, including during statement-taking (Section 36(6)(c))
- Right to silence and against self-incrimination — any statement obtained through oppression can later be excluded (Evidence Act 2011, Section 29(2))
- Right to humane treatment throughout (Section 34(1), ACJA Section 8)
- Right to be brought before a court within the legal time limit if not released (Section 35(4)-(5))

**What you can say:**
- "I want a lawyer present before I answer any questions."
- "I don't want to sign this statement until it's read back to me in a language I understand."
- "Am I entitled to police bail for this offence?"

## Stage: First Court Appearance (Arraignment)

**What happens:**
If the case proceeds, the suspect (now usually called the "defendant") is brought before a court, where the charge is formally read out and they're asked to enter a plea. This must be done in open court, in a language the defendant understands, and the defendant must be physically present — a lawyer can't be arraigned on their behalf in their absence.

**What you should expect:**
- Who will be there: a magistrate or judge, a prosecutor, court clerk/registrar, your lawyer (if you have one), and members of the public (proceedings are generally required to be public)
- What questions you'll be asked: whether you understand the charge, and how you plead (guilty or not guilty)
- What decisions might be made: whether you're granted bail at this stage, whether the case is adjourned to a later date, whether your plea is accepted as entered

**Your rights at this stage:**
- Right to be informed of the charge in detail and in a language you understand (Section 36(6)(a))
- Right to an interpreter (Section 36(6)(e))
- Right to a lawyer of your choice at this appearance (Section 36(6)(c))
- Right to a public hearing (Section 36(3)-(4))
- Right to apply for bail

**What you can say:**
- "I don't fully understand this charge — can it be explained again?"
- "I plead not guilty, and I want to apply for bail."
- "I need more time to get a lawyer before I enter a plea."

### Stage: Bail (Pending Trial)
 
**What happens:**
Whether at the police station or in court, the defendant can apply to be released while the case is ongoing, usually on conditions like a surety or a bond. The court weighs factors like the seriousness of the offence, flight risk, and whether the defendant is likely to interfere with the case. Bail conditions are not supposed to be so strict that they amount to keeping the person locked up anyway.
 
**What you should expect:**
- Who will be there: the judge/magistrate, prosecutor, your lawyer, and any proposed surety (someone vouching for you) if required
- What questions you'll be asked: about your ties to the community, your surety's ability to guarantee your return, sometimes your means to meet financial conditions
- What decisions might be made: bail granted unconditionally, bail granted with conditions (surety, reporting requirements, travel restrictions), or bail denied (more likely for very serious offences)
**Your rights at this stage:**
- General right to bail pending conviction, rooted in the presumption of innocence (Section 36(5))
- Right against unreasonably harsh bail conditions
- Right to have detention time limits respected if bail is delayed (Section 35(4)-(5))
**What you can say:**
- "I'd like to apply for bail — what conditions would the court consider reasonable?"
- "This bail condition isn't something I can realistically meet — can it be reviewed?"
- "I was asked to pay money directly for bail — I want to flag that as improper."


## Stage: Trial

**What happens:**
If a not-guilty plea stands, the case proceeds to trial. The prosecution presents its case first, calling witnesses and evidence; the defence can cross-examine each witness. After the prosecution closes, the defence may argue there's no case to answer, or proceed to present its own witnesses and evidence. Both sides then make final arguments before the court decides.

**What you should expect:**
- Who will be there: judge/magistrate, prosecutor, your lawyer, witnesses (called one at a time), court staff, and the public
- What questions you'll be asked: if you choose to testify, you'll be examined by your own lawyer and cross-examined by the prosecution — but you cannot be compelled to testify at all
- What decisions might be made: whether a "no case to answer" submission succeeds (ending the case early), what evidence is admitted, and ultimately guilt or innocence

**Your rights at this stage:**
- Right to a fair hearing by an independent, impartial court (Section 36(1))
- Right to a public trial within a reasonable time (Section 36(1), (3), (4))
- Right against self-incrimination — you cannot be compelled to give evidence (Section 36(11))
- Right to legal representation throughout (Section 36(6)(c))
- Right to an interpreter throughout (Section 36(6)(e))
- Right to challenge any confession obtained through oppression (Evidence Act 2011, Section 29(2))

**What you can say:**
- "I want this witness's statement explained to me before we continue."
- "I do not wish to testify."
- "I want to raise that this trial has been delayed unreasonably."

## Stage: Judgment & Sentencing

**What happens:**
After both sides have made their final arguments, the court delivers its decision — guilty or not guilty — and this must be announced in public. If convicted, sentencing may happen immediately or at a later date, depending on the offence and whether pre-sentencing reports are needed.

**What you should expect:**
- Who will be there: judge/magistrate, prosecutor, your lawyer, and the public (judgment delivered in chambers instead of open court has been held by Nigerian courts not to satisfy this requirement)
- What questions you'll be asked: your lawyer may be asked to make a plea in mitigation (arguing for a lighter sentence) before sentencing
- What decisions might be made: acquittal, conviction with a specific sentence, or in some cases alternatives like suspended sentencing or community service under ACJA's more restorative provisions

**Your rights at this stage:**
- Right to have judgment delivered in public (Section 36(3))
- Right to be presumed innocent until this point (Section 36(5))
- Right to appeal the outcome (Section 241, Section 243)
- Protection from being tried again for the same offence after conviction or acquittal (Section 36(9))

**What you can say:**
- "I want to formally note my intention to appeal this decision."
- "Can my lawyer address the court on sentencing before it's finalized?
  

## Stage: Appeal

**What happens:**
This is not a fresh trial — it's a review by a higher court of whether the law was applied correctly and whether the process was fair. Depending on the type of decision, you may have an automatic right to appeal, or you may need the court's permission first.

**Your rights at this stage:** and **what you can say** — see the dedicated Right to Appeal file for full detail, since this stage is covered in depth there already.
