### Right to Interpreter

**What it means:**
You have the right to understand what is happening to you at the police station and in court. If you don't speak or understand English well, the police or the court are required to provide someone to explain proceedings in a language you understand. Under Nigerian law, a statement you give without understanding the language it was taken in can be thrown out in court.

**When it applies:**
- At the police station, before and during questioning
- When the charge against you is read out for the first time
- At every subsequent court appearance where evidence, rulings, or questions are presented
- Before you are asked to sign or thumbprint any statement

**What you can say/do:**
- "I don't understand English well. I need an interpreter."
- "Can someone explain this in Yoruba / Hausa / Pidgin?"
- "I didn't understand what the judge/officer just said. Please explain it again."
- "I don't want to sign this statement until it's read back to me in a language I understand."

**Common problems:**
- Police proceed with questioning without first checking whether the suspect understands English
- Court sessions continue in English even when it's clear the accused doesn't follow
- People nod or say "yes" to avoid looking foolish, even when they haven't understood
- Suspects are pressured to sign statements they can't read, in a language they don't speak

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 36(6)(a) and (e) — right to be informed promptly in a language understood, and to have the free assistance of an interpreter
- Administration of Criminal Justice Act (ACJA) 2015, Section 6 — notification of cause of arrest and rights of suspect
- Source: https://nigerian-constitution.com/chapter-4/ | https://www.policinglaw.info/assets/downloads/2015_Administration_of_Criminal_Justice_Act.pdf 

### Right to Lawyer

**What it means:**
You have the right to speak to a lawyer and to have one present with you — whether or not you can afford one, you're entitled to seek legal counsel, and if you can't afford a lawyer you can request free legal aid from the Legal Aid Council or a legal NGO. Any statement taken from you without a lawyer present (if you asked for one) can be challenged in court.

**When it applies:**
- Immediately after arrest, before questioning begins
- While making any statement to the police
- At your first court appearance (arraignment)
- Throughout trial and any appeal

**What you can say/do:**
- "I wish to remain silent and I would like to speak to a lawyer."
- "I want a lawyer present before I answer any questions."
- "I cannot afford a lawyer. I want the Legal Aid Council to be contacted for me."
- "I will not sign anything until my lawyer has seen it."

**Common problems:**
- Police pressure suspects to "just talk" before a lawyer arrives, or discourage them from asking for one
- Suspects are told a lawyer isn't needed for a "small matter" or "just questioning"
- People don't know free legal aid exists and assume no lawyer means no rights
- Statements are taken and signed before a requested lawyer is given a chance to arrive

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 36(6)(c) — right to defend oneself in person or by a legal practitioner of one's choice
- Administration of Criminal Justice Act (ACJA) 2015 and state Administration of Criminal Justice Laws — right to make a statement to police in the presence of a lawyer of choice
- Legal Aid Act 2011 — establishes the Legal Aid Council for those who cannot afford counsel
- Source: https://nigerian-constitution.com/chapter-4/ | https://www.policinglaw.info/assets/downloads/2015_Administration_of_Criminal_Justice_Act.pdf



### Right to Silence

**What it means:**
You are not required to answer questions from the police or say anything that could incriminate you. You can wait until you've spoken to a lawyer before responding to any question. If you do say something, and it was obtained through pressure, threats, or oppressive treatment, it can be challenged and kept out of your trial entirely.

**When it applies:**
- The moment you're arrested or detained — before any questioning starts
- During police interrogation
- While a statement is being written down or recorded
- At trial — you cannot be forced to give evidence against yourself

**What you can say/do:**
- "I wish to remain silent until I've spoken to a lawyer."
- "I don't have to answer that."
- "I'm willing to give my name and address, but nothing further without my lawyer present."
- If a statement was taken under pressure: "That statement wasn't given freely — I want it challenged in court."

**Common problems:**
- Officers keep questioning after a suspect has clearly said they want to stay silent or wants a lawyer
- Suspects aren't told they have the right to silence before questioning begins
- Statements are obtained through threats, exhaustion, or promises, then presented in court as if freely given
- People assume staying silent will "look guilty," so they talk without a lawyer present

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 35(2) — right to remain silent or avoid answering questions until consultation with a legal practitioner
- Constitution, Section 36(11) — no person on trial for a criminal offence can be compelled to give evidence at their own trial
- Evidence Act 2011, Section 28 — defines what counts as a confession
- Evidence Act 2011, Section 29(2)-(3) — a confession is inadmissible if obtained by oppression or by anything likely to make it unreliable, unless the prosecution proves otherwise beyond reasonable doubt; the court can require this proof on its own initiative
- Evidence Act 2011, Section 31 — a confession isn't automatically excluded just because it followed a promise of secrecy, deception, intoxication, or a missed formal caution — relevance still applies (this limits how far the right protects you, so worth knowing)
- Source: https://cjad.nottingham.ac.uk/en/state/100/keyword/310 | https://blog.lawpavilion.com/the-new-tests-of-admissibility-of-confession-under-the-evidence-act-2011 | https://omaplex.com.ng/an-examination-of-the-nigerian-laws-on-confession

**Note:** The 2011 Evidence Act replaced the older "voluntariness" test for confessions with an oppression/unreliability test — legally more precise, but practitioners and even judges still often say "voluntary confession" out of habit. Don't build your content around the word "voluntary" as if it's still the legal standard.


###  Right to Bail

**What it means:**
Being arrested does not mean you must stay in custody until trial. Depending on the offence, you can be released — either by the police (police bail) or by a court (court bail) — often on conditions like a surety, an address, or a bond. Bail conditions are not supposed to be so harsh that they amount to keeping you locked up anyway. Bail is not a punishment and is not supposed to be sold — asking suspects or their families to pay police officers directly for bail is illegal ("bail is free").

**When it applies:**
- After arrest, before you're taken to court, for less serious offences (police bail)
- At your first court appearance, for most offences (court bail)
- While your case is ongoing, if circumstances change (bail pending trial or appeal)
- Note: some serious offences (e.g., capital offences like murder or treason) have bail restricted or made harder to obtain by law

**What you can say/do:**
- "Am I entitled to bail for this offence? On what conditions?"
- "This bail condition is impossible for me to meet — can it be reviewed?"
- "I was told to pay money for bail. I want to know if this is a bribe."
- "How long can I legally be held before I'm either charged, granted bail, or brought to court?"

**Common problems:**
- Police or court officials demand money directly for "bail" (this is illegal — bail itself is free; only bond/surety paperwork may apply)
- Suspects are held well beyond the legal time limit without being charged or brought before a court
- Bail conditions (e.g., requiring a landed-property surety) are set so high that poorer suspects can't meet them and stay in custody anyway
- Suspects don't know they can ask a court to review or vary bail conditions

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 35 — right to personal liberty and limits on detention; Section 36(5) — presumption of innocence, which underlies the general right to bail before conviction
- Administration of Criminal Justice Act (ACJA) 2015, Part 2 ("Arrest, Bail and Preventive Justice") — governs bail eligibility, conditions, and timelines for release or arraignment
- Source: https://nigerian-constitution.com/chapter-4/ | https://www.policinglaw.info/assets/downloads/2015_Administration_of_Criminal_Justice_Act.pdf

**Note:** ACJA 2015 is a federal law (applies to FCT and federal offences). States have their own Administration of Criminal Justice Laws (Lagos, Kaduna, Rivers, etc.) which may set different bail conditions — check the specific state law where the arrest happens.

### Right to Be Informed of Charges

**What it means:**
If you're arrested, you must be told promptly — and in a language you understand — exactly what you're accused of and why you're being held. This isn't optional or something the police can delay indefinitely; it must happen at or near the point of arrest, not hours or days later.

**When it applies:**
- At the moment of arrest
- Before or during any questioning
- At your first appearance in court, when the formal charge is read

**What you can say/do:**
- "What exactly am I being arrested for? Please tell me the specific offence."
- "I want this explained in a language I understand before I answer any questions."
- "Can I see the charge in writing?"

**Common problems:**
- People are arrested and held without being told a specific reason ("come to the station, we'll explain there")
- Vague accusations ("you know what you did") are given instead of a specific offence
- Suspects are questioned for hours before any charge is mentioned
- The charge changes later without the suspect being clearly informed of the change

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 35(3) — persons arrested must be informed in writing of the facts and grounds for arrest within 24 hours; Section 36(6)(a) — right to be informed promptly of the details of the offence charged
- Administration of Criminal Justice Act (ACJA) 2015, Section 6 — notification of cause of arrest and rights of suspect
- Source: https://nigerian-constitution.com/chapter-4/ | https://www.policinglaw.info/assets/downloads/2015_Administration_of_Criminal_Justice_Act.pdf   

### Right to Fair Hearing

**What it means:**
Anyone facing a court case — civil or criminal — is entitled to have that case heard within a reasonable time, by a court or tribunal that is genuinely independent and impartial, and to have the proceedings held in public. This is the umbrella right that everything else in a trial (interpreter, lawyer, being informed of the charge) sits under, but it also stands on its own: a trial can be technically "fair" in the sense of following procedure and still breach this right if it drags on for years, or if the judge/tribunal has a stake in the outcome.

**When it applies:**
- The entire duration of a criminal or civil case, from first appearance to final judgment
- Whenever a court or tribunal makes a decision affecting someone's rights or obligations
- To the venue and manner of the hearing itself — proceedings and the announcement of decisions must happen in public, not in private chambers
- To the composition of the court/tribunal — it must be set up in a way that secures independence and impartiality

**What you can say/do:**
- "This case has been delayed for years without progress — that's a breach of my right to a hearing within a reasonable time."
- "I don't believe this tribunal can be impartial, given [specific conflict of interest] — I want that raised on record."
- "This proceeding should be held in public. Why is it being conducted privately?"
- "The judgment/decision needs to be delivered in open court, not in chambers."

**Common problems:**
- Cases stall for years awaiting trial, especially where the accused is in custody, defeating the "reasonable time" requirement
- A judge, tribunal member, or panel has a personal or institutional stake in the outcome, undermining impartiality
- Proceedings or judgments are delivered in a judge's chambers rather than in open court, which courts have held does not satisfy "public" hearing
- Virtual/remote court sittings raise unresolved questions about what counts as "public" under this section

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 36(1) — right to fair hearing within a reasonable time, by a court/tribunal constituted to secure independence and impartiality
- Section 36(3) — proceedings and the announcement of decisions must be held in public
- Section 36(4) — anyone charged with a criminal offence is entitled to a fair hearing in public within a reasonable time, subject to narrow exceptions (defence, public safety/order/morality, protecting minors or private lives, or where publicity would harm the interests of justice)
- Related but separately covered elsewhere: Section 36(6) (informed of charge, lawyer, interpreter — see their own files); Section 36(11) (no compulsion to give evidence at own trial — see right-to-silence file)
- Source: https://nigerian-constitution.com/?p=512 | https://legalpediaonline.com/?p=3308 (Fabian Imoh v. The State, on "chambers" not satisfying "public")


### Right to Humane Treatment / Dignity of Person

**What it means:**
Every person is entitled to have their dignity respected, no matter what they're accused of. This means no torture, and no cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment — at the point of arrest, during questioning, or while in custody. This right applies unconditionally; it isn't something that can be suspended because someone is a suspect in a serious crime.

**When it applies:**
- During the arrest itself — no excessive force is permitted
- During police questioning or interrogation
- While in custody, including conditions of detention (food, access to medical care, physical treatment)
- At any point during the criminal justice process, including trial and post-conviction

**What you can say/do:**
- "I am being mistreated. This is a violation of my right to dignity under Section 34."
- "I need medical attention — withholding that is inhumane treatment."
- "Any statement obtained through force or threats against me should not be used in court."
- If witnessing this happen to someone else: report it to a human rights NGO or the National Human Rights Commission.

**Common problems:**
- Suspects are beaten or physically coerced during arrest or interrogation despite not resisting
- Poor detention conditions — overcrowding, inadequate food, denial of medical care
- Confessions extracted through torture or oppressive treatment are still sometimes presented as evidence
- Victims of custodial abuse often don't know this is a distinct, enforceable constitutional right, not just a moral wrong

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 34(1) — every individual is entitled to respect for the dignity of their person; no person shall be subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment
- Anti-Torture Act 2017 — makes torture a distinct criminal offence in Nigerian law, reinforcing Section 34 (I have not personally verified the specific section numbers of this Act — check the full text before citing specific provisions)
- Evidence Act 2011, Section 29(2) — a confession obtained through oppression is inadmissible in court, directly connecting this right to how mistreatment can affect a criminal case
- Source: https://omaplex.com.ng/?p=6768 | https://blog.lawpavilion.com/the-new-tests-of-admissibility-of-confession-under-the-evidence-act-2011

### Right to Appeal

**What it means:**
If you're convicted (or the prosecution disagrees with an acquittal), the case doesn't automatically end at the first court's decision. You — or in some cases the Attorney-General — can take the matter to a higher court to be reviewed. For certain serious outcomes, like a death sentence, this right applies automatically ("as of right"); for others, you may need the court's permission ("leave") first.

**When it applies:**
- After a final decision (conviction or sentence) at the Federal High Court or a State High Court — appeal goes to the Court of Appeal
- After a decision of the Court of Appeal — a further appeal can go to the Supreme Court, though this is more limited
- Automatically ("as of right"), without needing permission, when: the case was a final decision at first hearing, the appeal is purely about a point of law, it involves interpreting the Constitution, it involves a possible breach of a Chapter IV fundamental right, or the person was sentenced to death
- With the court's permission ("leave"), for other kinds of appeals not covered by the automatic categories

**What you can say/do:**
- "I want to appeal this decision — what court does it go to, and do I need leave or is this as-of-right?"
- "This appeal is purely on a point of law — I understand that qualifies as of right."
- "I was sentenced to death — I know I have an automatic right of appeal on that basis."
- "I can't afford legal representation for the appeal — can Legal Aid assist with this stage too?"

**Common problems:**
- People assume a first-court conviction is final and don't realize an appeal is possible at all
- Confusion between "as of right" appeals (automatic) and "with leave" appeals (permission needed) leads to missed deadlines or wrongly filed applications
- Appeal windows are time-limited — delays in deciding to appeal, or in finding a lawyer, can cause the right to lapse
- Victims/complainants sometimes wrongly believe they have the same automatic right of appeal as the accused when a case ends in acquittal — the law generally reserves this for the accused or the Attorney-General, with narrow court-recognized exceptions

**Legal basis:**
- Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended), Section 241(1) — lists the categories of appeal to the Court of Appeal available "as of right," including final decisions at first instance, pure questions of law, constitutional interpretation, Chapter IV fundamental-rights breaches, and death sentences
- Section 242 — governs appeals that require leave (permission) of the court
- Section 243(1)(a) — confirms the right of appeal in criminal proceedings belongs to the accused person, or the Attorney-General of the Federation/State
- Section 233(2) — sets out the equivalent categories of appeal, as of right, from the Court of Appeal to the Supreme Court
- Source: https://nigerian-constitution.com/?p=1450 | https://nigerian-constitution.com/?p=1413 | https://omaplex.com.ng/?p=34317 (judicial interpretation of leave to appeal, Akpan v. State)
